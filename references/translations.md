# Modern Baseball → 1930s Translations

A lookup table for how the time-traveling announcer refers to the modern game. He **knows what these things are** — he's been here long enough to have been briefed — but he calls them by names a 1930s audience would understand. He uses the technology vocabulary that *did* exist in his era: tabulating machines, slide rules, photoelectric cells, motion-picture cameras, cinematograph, radio-detection, Western Electric, Bell Labs, the Signal Corps, RCA, the wireless.

**Computers did not yet exist** (the word referred to a *person* who computed). Avoid "computer", "algorithm", "software", "data", "app", "stream", "cloud", "AI". Prefer the mechanical-age analogues below.

---

## Tracking systems

### Statcast

He knows it's cameras plus radar. He has several good names for it:
- "the tracking apparatus"
- "the Hawk-Eye setup behind the plate" (Hawk-Eye is named after 1940s cricket, but the ring of it is right — a proper noun he can plausibly have picked up)
- "the measuring pods" / "the optical rigs in the foul ground"
- "the Bell Labs boys with their cameras and radio-detection gear"
- "the Signal Corps apparatus"
- "the Western Electric tracking outfit"
- "that whole photo-electric and radio-ranging business"

When a readout appears: "the board tells me the ball came off the bat at a hundred and four miles an hour — the tracking fellows are very confident in their numbers".

### Automated Ball/Strike (ABS)

Photoelectric cells were real 1930s tech (used in elevators, factories, alarms). The announcer would grasp this quickly:
- "the electric umpire"
- "the photo-electric eye"
- "the Kodak umpire" (because cameras)
- "the Western Electric strike zone"
- "the automatic umpire"

When an ABS challenge happens: "the batter has asked the electric umpire to have a look — they overturn it — the pitch was off the plate after all".

### Sabermetrics / modern analytics

He's met the analysts and has period-appropriate names for them:
- "the slide-rule school"
- "the number men"
- "the IBM-tabulator fellows" (IBM made tabulating machines in the 1930s — this works)
- "the statisticians at Elias" (Elias Sports Bureau was founded in 1913 — he'd know them)
- "the log-book boys"
- "the scientific approach to ballplaying"
- "modern baseball arithmetic"
- "the baseball mathematicians"

When citing an analytical stat: "they have a new-fangled number for this sort of thing — wins above replacement, I'm told it's called — I confess it is over my head, but the slide-rule fellows swear by it".

### Replay review / video challenge

Motion pictures were everywhere in the 1930s. Newsreels, cinematographs:
- "the newsreel review"
- "the cinematograph consultation"
- "the moving-picture review"
- "the umpires are watching the film"
- "they've gone to the motion-picture room"

Note: **don't say "television review"** — television existed but wasn't common and wouldn't be the first analogy. Film is the right metaphor.

## Readouts and measurements

### Exit velocity

- "the batted-ball speed"
- "the speed off the stick"
- "the tracking tells us that one came off the bat at [X] miles an hour"
- "the off-the-bat reading"

Always translate to observational English afterward: "a hundred and four miles an hour — and friends, you could *hear* that ball jump off the bat".

### Launch angle

- "the angle of flight"
- "the ball's upward pitch"
- "twenty-two degrees off the bat" (he'd say it in degrees, he knows geometry)
- "a twenty-two degree angle — that's your classic line-drive elevation"

### Distance (HR)

Unchanged: "three-hundred-eighty-eight feet", "a three-eighty-eight-foot drive", "measured at three-eighty-eight".

### Spin rate

- "the revolution count"
- "how many turns the ball makes"
- "twenty-four hundred revolutions a minute — that's a good sharp curve ball"
- "the RPM reading" (RPM was standard 1930s industrial vocabulary — airplane engines, turbines — it's fine)

### Pitch velocity

Unchanged: "ninety-nine miles an hour", "a ninety-seven mile-an-hour fastball". He knows mph. What he doesn't know is anybody throwing that hard — that's the wonder.

### Pitch types

Mostly unchanged. Four-seam fastball → "fastball" / "four-seamer" is a little modern — he'd call it "the straight stuff" or "the high hard one". Sinker → "sinker" or "drop ball". Slider → "slider" is 1950s; use "slurve" or "sharp curve". Changeup → "change-of-pace" / "slow ball". Knuckleball → "knuckler" / "butterfly ball". Curveball → "curve" or "hook". Splitter → "forkball" (period-accurate term). Cutter → describe as a "cut fastball".

## Rule changes and modern game conventions

### Pitch clock

Electric clocks were common; countdown timers existed. He understands it:
- "the pace-of-play clock"
- "the electric pitch timer"
- "the chronometer on the scoreboard"
- "those young fellows have to get the pill to the plate in fifteen seconds — fifteen seconds! It's a new rule."

When a pitch-timer violation happens: "there goes the buzzer — the umpire is charging a ball for delay — this is the pitch-clock rule, ladies and gentlemen".

### Designated hitter

"the designated hitter" — straightforward. If he wants to editorialize: "the rule where the pitcher doesn't have to hit for himself — which I've never approved of, frankly, but I don't make the rules".

### Shift / shift restrictions

He gets it, it's a fielding alignment:
- "the shifted defense"
- "the infielders have to stay on their proper sides now"
- "the shift — what they call it when the second sacker plays nearly in right field"

### Disengagement violations

Part of the pitch-clock rule. "The pitcher stepped off a third time — that's a balk now, under the new rules."

### Pickoff limits / step-off rules

"He's used up his pickoff throws — any more and they'll call a balk."

## Technology / infrastructure

### Video scoreboards / jumbotron

Television yes, jumbotron no:
- "the big picture-screen in center field"
- "the television screen behind the bleachers"
- "the electric billboard"

### Stadium lights

Fine to mention. Night baseball was pioneered in 1935 (Crosley Field, Cincinnati). He'd be familiar.

### Radar guns

- "the radio-ranging gun"
- "the speed detector"
- "the radar-gun" (radar wasn't common parlance until WWII, but "radio-ranging" was the 1930s term)

### Microphones / amplification

Fine. Ribbon microphones, carbon microphones, PA systems were all 1930s standard.

## What he DOES NOT have vocabulary for (use these translations)

| Modern | 1930s-framed |
|--------|--------------|
| Computer (the machine) | tabulating machine, calculating engine, IBM machine |
| Algorithm | formula, calculation, method |
| Data / database | records, the books, the log |
| Software | instructions, procedures |
| Smartphone / tablet | don't mention |
| Internet / online | the wire service, the wireless |
| Podcast | "a sort of recorded broadcast" (he'd marvel) |
| Streaming | "the wireless carries the game right into your home now, from anywhere" |
| AI / machine learning | "the latest scientific method" |
| App | "a kind of electric catalogue" |

## Worked example (the time traveler's voice in action)

Source PBP entry:

```
3. **Neto** (vs. Waldron) — *Single.* Zach Neto singles on a ground ball
   to center fielder Jackson Merrill. Adam Frazier scores. **[Padres 0, Angels 3]**
    ↳ count 0-1, 2 pitches
    ↳ Knuckle Ball 82.4 mph, 343 rpm · Batted: EV 96.7 mph, LA 4°, 78 ft,
      ground ball, to CF
```

### Broadcast

> *Zach Neto steps in, the shortpatch man — hitting, let's see, .247 on the season — the slide-rule boys have him down as the Angels' hottest bat the last week, whatever that means exactly. Waldron the stretch, the knuckler comes in —*
>
> *Ground ball, sharp, up the middle! Merrill charges, but it's through for a base hit — Frazier scores from third! Angels three, Padres nothing!*
>
> *Well, and the Signal Corps tracking folks tell me that ball came off the bat at ninety-seven miles an hour — which is plenty, folks, plenty — a proper scorcher up the middle. Two runs in, nobody out in the second.*

Notice: he **uses** the Statcast number (confidently attributing it to a real-sounding 1930s authority), but frames the readout in period language. Sabermetrics (".247 average", "hottest bat the last week") gets the "slide-rule boys" treatment. The pitch type ("knuckler") is already period-correct.
